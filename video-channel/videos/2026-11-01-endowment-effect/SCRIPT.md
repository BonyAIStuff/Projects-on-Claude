## 4 · Chapter 1 — The mug that doubled in price (0:25–2:15)
[CHAPTER CARD: 01 · The mug that doubled in price]

**Analogy — SAY:** "Think about a rented flat versus your own flat. Same walls. Same tiles. But the day you hang your family photo on that wall, a cracked tile stops being 'the landlord's problem'. It becomes *my* tile. Nothing changed about the tile. Only one thing changed. It feels like yours."
[ANIM: a flat outline; a photo frame slides onto the wall; the label on a tile flips from "landlord's" to "MINE" with a highlighter sweep]

**How it really works — SAY:** "Economists call this the endowment effect. Once something feels like ours, we value it more. Because giving it up now feels like a loss. And our brains hate losses more than they love equal gains."
[SLIDE: two-sided scale — "Gain ₹100" on one side, "Lose ₹100" on the other; the loss side drops lower] [TEXT CARD: "Endowment effect = it's mine, so it's worth more"]

**Proof — SAY:** "In 1990, three researchers, one of them later a Nobel winner, ran a simple test at Cornell University. They gave plain coffee mugs to half the students, at random. Then they asked: owners, what would you sell it for? Everyone else, what would you pay? The owners wanted about seven dollars. The buyers offered about three. Same mug. More than double the price. Just because it was in their hands. (R1)"
[ANIM: classroom split in two; mugs drop onto half the desks] [STAT: ~$7 vs ~$3 · Sellers vs buyers, same mug · Source: Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler, J. Political Economy, 1990] [ANNOTATE: red circle on "$7"] [SFX: ping]

**Objection — SAY:** "Now you might be thinking: maybe the owners just liked mugs more? That's the clever bit. The mugs were handed out at random. Owners and buyers were the same kind of students. The only difference was who was holding one. To be fair, later researchers showed part of that gap comes from how the test was run. But the effect keeps showing up, and it's strongest for things you can touch."
[TEXT CARD: "Maybe the owners just liked mugs more?"]

**Re-hook — SAY:** "Here's the crazy part. You don't need to own it. You don't even need to hold it for long. (F3)"
[SFX: soft hit] [PUNCH-IN]
[REEL 1: 0:25–1:35 · hook card: "Same mug. Double the price."]

## 4 · Chapter 2 — Thirty seconds in your hand (2:15–4:30)
[CHAPTER CARD: 02 · Thirty seconds in your hand]

**Analogy — SAY:** "Ever held a friend's newborn baby? For the first ten seconds, you're careful. By thirty seconds, you don't want to give the baby back. That's not just love. That's your brain filing it under 'mine'."
[ANIM: simple illustrated arms holding a swaddled baby; a timer ticks 0:10 → 0:30; a small "mine" tag appears] 

**How it really works — SAY:** "Researchers tested exactly this. People held a mug for either ten seconds or thirty seconds, then bid for it in a real auction. The thirty-second group bid significantly more. (R3) Another study found that just touching a product raises how much you feel you own it, and how much you'll pay for it. Even when it's not yours. (R2)"
[SLIDE: timeline bar 10s vs 30s, the 30s bar's bid arrow taller] [STAT: 30 seconds · Enough touch to raise your bid · Source: Wolf, Arkes & Muhanna, 2008] [SFX: ping]

**Proof — SAY:** "Now walk into an Apple store. Every iPhone, every Mac, every iPad is out. Unlocked. No security box. Staff don't rush you. Retail watchers say even the laptop screens are tilted to the same angle every morning, so you reach out and fix them. (F1) And it works. In 2023, each Apple store in India was reported to be selling twenty-two to twenty-five crore rupees a month. (R8)"
[B-ROLL: Apple BKC / Saket exterior and interior — licensed stock or self-shot] [STAT: ₹22–25 cr · Monthly sales per Apple India store (2023) · Source: MyMobileIndia, 2023] [ANNOTATE: arrow to "per month"]

**SAY:** "Decathlon does the same thing, but bigger. Cricket nets. Football cages. Basketball courts. Inside the store. You don't just touch the bat. You hit three shots with it. And now it's *your* bat. (N4)"
[B-ROLL: Decathlon India store play zone — licensed stock or self-shot] [NEWS N4: Medianews4u, highlight: "use our play areas… cannot be fully replicated online"]

**Objection — SAY:** "But wait. If touch sells so well, everyone would do it, right? Here's the catch: it's expensive. Decathlon India made over four thousand crore in revenue last year. But it posted a loss of sixty-five crore. (R9) Giant stores with sports courts cost a lot. So touch isn't free money. It's a bet that those thirty seconds pay for the floor space."
[TEXT CARD: "If touch works, why doesn't every shop do it?"] [NEWS N3: Entrackr, highlight: "Rs 65 cr loss"] [STAT: ₹4,133 cr revenue · ₹65 cr loss · Decathlon India FY25 · Source: Entrackr (RoC filings)] [ANNOTATE: red circle on "₹65 cr loss"]

**Re-hook — SAY:** "And this trick is so old, a government once warned shoppers about it. In 2003, the Illinois Attorney General in the US warned holiday shoppers: watch out for salespeople who ask you to hold the product and imagine owning it. (F2)"
[TEXT CARD: "Don't let them put it in your hands." — 2003 warning, Illinois AG] [SFX: typing]

## 4 · Chapter 3 — Your phone fakes the touch (4:30–6:30)
[CHAPTER CARD: 03 · Your phone fakes the touch]

**Analogy — SAY:** "So what about online shopping? You can't touch a lipstick through Wi-Fi. Or can you? Think of it like a trial room mirror. The clothes aren't yours yet. But the mirror shows them on *you*. That's all online stores need."
[ANIM: a trial-room mirror; a kurta appears on a silhouette; the mirror morphs into a phone screen]

**How it really works — SAY:** "The same study that showed touch builds ownership found something else. Just *imagining* you own it works too. And the researchers said it plainly: that's how online sellers can sell without touch. (R2) So apps fake three things. Touch. Use. And imagining it's yours."
[SLIDE: three columns — TOUCH (swipe, zoom) · USE (try-on, view in room) · IMAGINE ("Your bag", "Saved for you")]

**Proof — SAY:** "Start with touch. In one study, people shopping on a touchscreen valued products about fifty percent more than people using a mouse. Your finger on the glass is almost a hand on the product. (R4) Then use. Myntra says its virtual makeup try-on doubled conversions in makeup. (R11) And in a survey for Snapchat, seventy-two percent of shoppers said they're more likely to buy after trying something on in AR. (R13)"
[ANIM: a finger swipes a sweatshirt on a phone; price tag inflates] [STAT: ~50% · Higher value on touchscreen vs mouse · Source: Brasel & Gips, via Boston College] [SFX: ping] [STAT: 2× · Makeup conversions with virtual try-on · Source: Myntra (company figure)] [STAT: 72% · More likely to buy after AR try-on · Source: Snap × Publicis survey, 2022]

**SAY:** "And then imagine. Look at the words. Not 'cart'. *Your* bag. *Your* wishlist. *Saved for you.* The app is telling you it's already yours."
[ANNOTATE: red-pen underline on "Your" in a mock app header "Your Bag (2)"] [SFX: pen scratch]

**Objection — SAY:** "Now you might be thinking: if apps can fake it, why are shops still around? Because faking it has a bill. In Indian online fashion, around thirty to thirty-five percent of orders come back. And over forty percent of those returns are about size. (R14) That's the cost of not letting you touch."
[TEXT CARD: "If apps can fake it, why do stores exist?"] [STAT: 30–35% · Fashion & footwear orders returned in India · Source: Return Prime, 2024] [ANNOTATE: circle "30–35%"]

**Re-hook — SAY:** "But here's where it gets interesting. Amazon shut down its 'try before you buy' service in January 2025. (F4) Warby Parker, the brand famous for posting you five frames to try at home, is ending that too. (F5) Both are betting that AI try-on is now good enough. The fake touch is replacing the real one."
[NEWS: eMarketer, "Warby Parker … ends home try-on, bets on stores and eye exams"] [SFX: whoosh]
